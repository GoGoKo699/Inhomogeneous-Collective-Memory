# Exact interaction algebra and finite-size relaxation

For pairwise distinct coefficients in $`[1,2]`$, the register has no nontrivial noiseless subsystem for its interaction algebra. This note proves that assertion and specifies what it does—and does not—say about storage time. The approximate storage construction is proved separately in [storage_theorem.md](storage_theorem.md).

## 1. Statement and algebra conventions

Use the model of [model.md](model.md):

```math
A_x=\sum_{j=1}^n g_jX_j,\qquad
A_z=\sum_{j=1}^n g_jZ_j,\qquad
\mathcal L_n
=-\frac{\gamma_x}{2}[A_x,[A_x,\cdot]]
 -\frac{\gamma_z}{2}[A_z,[A_z,\cdot]],
```

with $`\gamma_x,\gamma_z>0`$.

**Proposition.** If all $`g_j`$ are nonzero and the numbers $`g_j^2`$ are pairwise distinct, then:

1. The real Lie algebra generated by $`iA_x,iA_z`$ is
   $`\bigoplus_{j=1}^n\mathfrak{su}(2)_j`$.
2. The unital complex associative $`*`$-algebra generated by $`A_x,A_z`$ is
   $`\mathcal B((\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes n})`$.
3. The common commutant of $`A_x,A_z`$ consists only of scalar multiples of the identity.
4. The kernel of $`\mathcal L_n`$ consists only of scalar operators, and

   ```math
   \lim_{t\to\infty}\Phi_{n,t}(O)
   =\frac{\operatorname{Tr}O}{2^n}I
   \qquad\text{for every operator }O.
   ```

Pairwise distinct values in $`[1,2]`$ satisfy the hypotheses. In particular, they hold for $`g_j=1+j/n`$.

The distinction between the Lie and associative algebras matters. Commutators of sums of one-spin terms remain sums of one-spin terms, so the Lie algebra is not $`\mathfrak{su}(2^n)`$ when $`n>1`$. Products of the recovered one-spin operators generate the full matrix algebra.

## 2. Recovering individual spins by a Vandermonde argument

Define the weighted one-spin operators

```math
X^{(r)}=\sum_jg_j^rX_j,\qquad
Y^{(r)}=\sum_jg_j^rY_j,\qquad
Z^{(r)}=\sum_jg_j^rZ_j.
```

Different sites commute. The Pauli commutation relations therefore give

```math
[Z^{(1)},X^{(r)}]=2iY^{(r+1)},\qquad
[Z^{(1)},[Z^{(1)},X^{(r)}]]=4X^{(r+2)}.
```

Starting from $`X^{(1)}=A_x`$ and repeatedly commuting with $`Z^{(1)}=A_z`$ produces, up to nonzero scalar factors,

```math
X^{(1)},X^{(3)},\ldots,X^{(2n-1)}.
```

Their coefficient matrix is

```math
V_{rj}=g_j^{2r+1},\qquad 0\le r\le n-1,
```

with determinant

```math
\det V
=\left(\prod_{j=1}^n g_j\right)
  \prod_{1\le i<j\le n}(g_j^2-g_i^2),
```

up to the immaterial convention for the order of rows and columns. It is nonzero under the stated hypotheses. Hence every individual $`X_j`$ is a linear combination of these weighted operators. In the real skew-Hermitian convention, the same calculation yields every $`iX_j`$ in the generated Lie algebra.

Next,

```math
[A_z,X_j]=2ig_jY_j,\qquad
[X_j,Y_j]=2iZ_j.
```

Since $`g_j\ne0`$, the Lie algebra contains $`iX_j,iY_j,iZ_j`$ for every site. Conversely, all original generators and every commutator lie in the direct sum of these local Lie algebras. This proves the first claim.

The associative algebra contains products of the individual Pauli matrices. Those products include every Pauli string and span the complete operator space. This proves the second claim. An operator commuting with the generators commutes with their commutators, linear combinations, and products, hence with every matrix. It must therefore be scalar, proving the third claim.

## 3. Fixed operators and convergence

Equip the operator space with the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product
$`\langle O_1,O_2\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(O_1^\dagger O_2)`$.
For Hermitian $`A`$,

```math
\langle O,\mathcal D[A](O)\rangle
=-\frac12\|[A,O]\|_2^2.
```

Consequently,

```math
\langle O,\mathcal L_n(O)\rangle
=-\frac{\gamma_x}{2}\|[A_x,O]\|_2^2
 -\frac{\gamma_z}{2}\|[A_z,O]\|_2^2.
```

Both coefficients are positive. Thus

```math
\ker\mathcal L_n
=\{O:[A_x,O]=[A_z,O]=0\}
=\mathbb CI.
```

The generator is self-adjoint and negative semidefinite on this finite-dimensional inner-product space. It has an orthonormal eigenbasis, with one zero eigenvalue and all remaining eigenvalues strictly negative. Exponentiating gives convergence to the orthogonal projection onto the identity, which is precisely the completely depolarizing limit in the proposition.

## 4. Interpretation for noiseless storage

In the interaction-algebra description of noiseless subsystems, quantum information resides in a multiplicity factor on which every interaction operator acts as identity. A full matrix algebra has no multiplicity factor of dimension greater than one. The proposition therefore excludes a nontrivial exact noiseless subsystem for these couplings. The interaction-algebra formulation is standard; a primary reference is [Knill, Laflamme, and Viola, *Theory of Quantum Error Correction for General Noise* (1999)](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9908066).

The same conclusion can be read from the semigroup: an exactly preserved logical coherence would supply a nondecaying operator mode. Here the only such mode is the identity. Because this generator is self-adjoint, it also has no nonzero imaginary eigenvalues that could sustain a unitary logical evolution.

These statements do not bound the relaxation rate uniformly in $`n`$. The Vandermonde inversion can become badly conditioned as coefficients approach each other, and the proposition makes no estimate of the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of $`-\mathcal L_n`$. It would be incorrect to infer a constant memory lifetime from the scalar commutant.

The storage theorem exploits precisely this distinction: a sequence of finite registers may have a full associative interaction algebra at every size while admitting increasingly good approximate codes over increasingly long intervals. It does not claim that the exact symmetry reappears at a finite size, or that its achieved storage time is optimal.

If coefficients coincide, the algebraic proposition need not apply. Exactly collective groups can then have exact singlet codes, as in [Zanardi and Rasetti (1997)](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9705044). The storage theorem still applies because it does not require distinct coefficients. Some such special lists permit indefinite exact storage; this is another reason an achievable lower bound is not a universal upper bound on lifetime.
